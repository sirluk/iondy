As I am nearing the end of my PhD, I finally have a bit of space to step back and reflect on the last few years. When you are in full grind mode (trying to get papers accepted while still making time for family and friends), there is rarely room to pause and look at how you are actually spending your days.

Before starting my PhD, I was an avid runner. I went through plenty of phases where I followed a strict training plan, sometimes with more running sessions scheduled in a week than there were days. Running was simply part of my routine.

## The PhD triangle

Once the PhD started, that changed quickly. Every hour suddenly felt so precious that if I wasn't using it to push my work forward or spend time with the people close to me, it felt like wasted time.

To me it feels like doing a PhD can be reduced to the famous "pick two" triangle:

- Make PhD progress
- Take care of relationships (family and friends)
- Take care of yourself (sport)

If you are incredibly disciplined, you can at most do two and a half. Looking back, I picked the first two, and my fitness took a big hit.

## Time to think

What took me a while to appreciate is that running doesn't just compete with the other two corners. Beyond the positive impact on your health, having 30 minutes to an hour in a busy day completely to yourself and your own thoughts is valuable on its own. No Slack messages, no paper deadlines, no screens.

Surprisingly, running also ended up helping me make actual progress on my PhD.

Running is an effective way of clearing your head. When you are out moving at a steady pace, problems that felt stuck at a desk have room to untangle themselves. On almost every run I did, one or more genuinely useful ideas came up, like how to structure a section in a paper, a small ablation worth running, or a new research question altogether.

That changes the math of the triangle a bit. Taking an hour to run shouldn't be seen as an hour not available for research. Often it was the hour that made the next few hours at the desk much more productive.

## Looking back

Now that the end of my PhD is in sight, running is part of my regular routine again (not with a strict training plan like before, just a few runs a week). Trying to force a strict training plan like that on top of deadlines and relationships probably isn't sustainable, but keeping a modest routine of 30 to 60 minutes a few times a week is, as it contributes to your own well-being and the quality of your thinking. If I had realized earlier how much running gave back to my work, I would have protected that time a lot more deliberately.

## Final thoughts

I noticed this effect most clearly with running, though I suspect it transfers to any endurance sport where you can be alone with your thoughts for an extended stretch, like cycling, swimming, rowing, or a long hike.
